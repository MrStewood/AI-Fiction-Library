# Creative Brief

## One-sentence concept

A potion maker who trades emotions for memory must confront her own grief when a customer asks her to destroy the last feeling he has for his dead wife — forcing both to decide whether pain is the price of love or its proof.

## Decision Log

|---|---|---|---|---|
| DEC-001 | Unspoken event class | Pre-existing loss. The potion maker's own unresolved grief (she lost someone years ago; she never speaks of it directly) is the hidden pressure that makes the customer's request land with professional and personal weight. This is the unspoken event underpinning her resistance and eventual shift. | The brief is intimate, two-person, no subplots. The unspoken event must live in what the protagonist already carries — not a new external event. | Considered: the customer's dead wife as the unspoken event (too external, belongs to him); a past shop disaster (too contrived for the scope); a time limit / closing shop (unnecessary pressure the brief doesn't need). |
| DEC-002 | "The box" framing | The potion itself (specifically the final grief-dissolution potion) is the catalyst/pressure source — not the shop, not the ingredients. The potion is a material object whose existence forces the question: *do you use it or refuse it?* The pressure is relational: the potion maker has the power to grant the request, and the cost is her own complicity. | The brief frames the potion as the mechanism. Making the shop or ingredients the "box" would dilute the central question. | Considered: framing the shop as the box (too diffuse — the shop is a setting, not a pressure source); framing the customer's grief as the box (the customer's grief is what he wants to surrender, not the catalyst for the protagonist's choice). |

## Format and target length

- **Format:** Short story
- **Profile:** `short_story`
- **Target word count:** 4,500
- **Word range:** 4,000–5,000
- **Chapters required:** No
- **Scenes:** 4–6 (target 5)

## Audience

Literary fantasy readers who enjoy contemplative, character-driven stories with grounded magic systems. Readers of Kelly Link, Carmen Maria Machado, Sofia Samatar — people who want emotional resonance and moral texture, not plot mechanics.

## Genre and story promise

**Genre:** Literary fantasy (low fantasy, no epic scope, grounded magic)

**Story promise:** The reader is promised a complete moral decision with lasting consequence — not a lesson, not a resolution, but a choice that changes both characters permanently. The magic system will have clear rules and costs. The ending will be hard and honest.

## Tone and emotional experience

- **Tone:** Contemplative, intimate, melancholic but not bleak. The story should feel like a late-evening conversation that goes further than either person intended.
- **Emotional arc:** Opens with professional distance (the potion maker is working); shifts to reluctant engagement (the customer's request is specific and personal); escalates to moral confrontation (the cost is named); closes with irreversible choice.
- **Restraint:** No sentimentality, no villain, no clean moral. The PG-13 constraint applies — emotional violence, not physical.

## POV and tense

- **POV:** Close third-person, focused on the potion maker (Maren)
- **Tense:** Past tense
- **Scope:** We only see what Maren sees, hears what she hears. The customer's interiority is limited to what his words and actions reveal.

## Content limits

- **Rating:** PG-13
- **Graphic content:** Non-graphic. Emotional intensity is permitted; physical violence, explicit sex, or gore are not.
- **Language:** No restriction on mild profanity if character-appropriate, but the tone favors restraint.

## General prose attributes

- Sentence-level craft: varied syntax, concrete nouns, active verbs.
- Sensory detail earns its place — never decorative.
- Dialogue carries weight through what is *not* said.
- The prose should feel close, intimate — the reader is in the shop, near the counter.

## Comparable qualities (no living-author imitation)

- The emotional precision of Kelly Link's short fiction
- The moral ambiguity of Sofia Samatar's fantasy
- The grounded magic of Ursula K. Le Guin's *Earthsea* (rules, costs, no easy answers)
- The compressed intimacy of a Raymond Carver story, but in a fantasy register

## Explicit assumptions

1. Maren (the potion maker) has her own history of loss that she has never processed. She does not name it. It lives in her behavior — how she handles certain ingredients, how she pauses before certain requests, how she keeps the shop running.
2. Elias (the customer) is not asking for a general forgetting. He wants to forget one specific grief — the loss of his wife. His wife died recently enough that the grief is raw, but long enough ago that he has tried everything else.
3. The potion has one rule: to forget an emotion, you must surrender it entirely. There is no partial version. You cannot choose which memories the emotion is attached to — it goes, and the memories become flat, factual, emptied of weight.
4. Maren has the ability to refuse the request. She is not compelled. Her choice to make or not make the potion is the story's engine.
5. The shop is a real place with real inventory — not a metaphor. The potions have names, prices, and clear effects.

## Research needs

- None required for this story. The world is self-contained.
- If additional sensory detail is needed: herbalism references for potion-making textures and smells (lavender, ash, charcoal, vinegar — grounded, non-precious ingredients).

## Definition of done

1. All required elements (REQ-001 through REQ-010) are addressed in the brief.
2. All forbidden elements (FBD-001 through FBD-007) are explicitly excluded.
3. The unspoken event class is locked in one sentence (DEC-001).
4. "The box" is framed as catalyst/pressure source, not puzzle inventory or MacGuffin (DEC-002).
5. The brief provides enough specificity for a writer to draft the story without additional inventing.
