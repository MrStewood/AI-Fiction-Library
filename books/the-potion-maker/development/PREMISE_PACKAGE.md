# Premise Package

## Logline

When a grieving widower visits a potion maker to erase the last emotional bond to his dead wife, she must decide whether to sell him the potion — knowing that if he drinks it, the memories will survive but the love that gave them weight will not.

## Short pitch

Maren runs a shop that trades in emotions: you bring what aches, she brews what dulls it. Elias arrives one evening wanting to forget his wife — not the facts of her, but the love that makes the facts unbearable. The potion she can make him will work. But Maren recognizes something in the request that mirrors her own unspoken loss, and the professional distance she's maintained for years begins to crack. The story is the conversation across that counter, the slow negotiation between two people who both know what grief costs and what forgetting costs, ending on the choice neither can undo.

## Central dramatic question

Will Maren make the potion for Elias — and if she does, will she be freeing him or destroying the last thing that proves he loved?

## External goal and failure consequences

**External goal:** Elias wants the grief-dissolution potion. Maren has the skill and ingredients to make it.

**If she makes it:** Elias loses the emotional weight of every memory of his wife. The facts remain — her name, her face, the life they shared — but they become inventory, no different from the other bottles on Maren's shelves. He walks out lighter and emptier.

**If she refuses:** Elias leaves carrying everything he came in with. No relief. The grief remains, unaltered, and Maren must live with the knowledge that she had the power to help and chose not to.

**Failure consequence either way:** Maren confronts the parallel between Elias's request and her own abandoned grief. Either choice forces her to acknowledge what she has been avoiding — that her professional expertise in managing emotion is itself a form of avoidance.

## Internal need / wound / misbelief

**Need:** Maren needs to stop treating grief as a problem to be solved and accept it as evidence of love — for her客户, and for herself.

**Wound:** Maren lost someone important years ago. She never named the loss publicly. Instead, she built a career around helping others manage theirs, which allowed her to keep her own grief at a professional distance. The wound is not dramatic; it is the quiet kind — a drawer she doesn't open, a shelf in the shop she keeps but never touches.

**Misbelief:** "Grief is damage. Helping someone neutralize it is kindness." Maren has built her livelihood on this belief. Elias's request will test whether she still holds it when the stakes are personal.

## Primary opposition

The opposition is the potion itself — a material object whose existence forces the question of whether to use it. There is no villain. Elias is not the antagonist; his grief is not the antagonist. The antagonist is the choice embedded in the transaction: the potion exists, it works, and using it is irreversible. Maren's professional identity is aligned with making it. Her personal history is aligned with refusing it. The opposition lives in the space between those two positions, inside her.

## Story engine (why this sustains target length)

The story is a conversation with escalating stakes. Each exchange between Maren and Elias moves the negotiation forward and forces new information into the open:

- Scene 1: Professional distance. Elias arrives; Maren assesses the request. The reader learns the rules of the magic system through how Maren works — what she asks, what she weighs, what she sets aside. The engine is curiosity: what is this potion, and what does it cost?

- Scene 2: The cost is named. Maren explains that surrendering grief means surrendering the emotional weight of the memories. Elias already knows this. The engine shifts: why does he still want it, and why does Maren hesitate?

- Scene 3: The parallel surfaces. Maren's behavior reveals her own loss. The engine becomes personal: Maren is no longer just a professional; she is someone who understands what Elias is asking for, because she has considered asking for it herself.

- Scene 4: The choice. Maren makes or refuses the potion. The engine is resolution: what will she do, and what will it cost?

The story sustains itself because each scene narrows the available options and deepens the emotional stakes. The magic system provides concrete, sensory stakes (ingredients, textures, the physical act of making or not making the potion). The dialogue provides subtextual stakes (what is said, what is avoided, what finally surfaces). There is no need for subplots or external events — the conversation is the plot.

## Thematic question

Is grief the price of love, or is it damage that love leaves behind?

## Distinctive story elements

- **Emotion as material commodity:** The potion shop treats feelings as tradeable goods with clear prices and effects. This is not metaphor — it is the literal economy of the world.
- **The cost is the cure:** You cannot numb grief without numbing the love that produced it. The potion does not distinguish.
- **Professional distance as avoidance:** Maren's career is itself a coping mechanism — a way to be near grief without owning her own.
- **Two-person pressure cooker:** The entire story happens in conversation, across a counter, in one evening. The constraints are the engine.

## Genre promise

Literary fantasy with the emotional precision of a character study. The reader is promised a complete, grounded magic system with real costs; a moral decision that does not resolve into a lesson; and an ending that is honest rather than comforting. The fantasy element (emotion potions) is not spectacle — it is the mechanism that forces the question the story actually cares about.

## Candidate notes

- **Selected candidate:** The potion as the central pressure object. Maren's choice to make or refuse it is the story's spine. The two-person, one-evening constraint is maintained.
- **Rejected alternatives summary:**
  - *The shop itself as the central object:* Too diffuse. The shop is a setting, not a pressure source.
  - *The customer's grief as the central object:* This belongs to Elias. The story's engine is Maren's choice, not his suffering.
  - *A time-pressure variant (shop closing, deadline):* Unnecessary. The brief does not need external pressure — the conversation is enough.
