# The Potion Maker

Seventeen copper, two silver. Maren counted the coins twice before dropping them into the tin beneath the register. The clink of metal against metal was the only sound in the shop. Outside, dusk had settled over the street — cobblestones gone purple in the fading light, the last of the day's traffic thinning to nothing. The man who'd bought the forgetfulness tonic was already gone, his shuffle fading around the corner like breath on glass.

A half-measure. He'd wanted the edges softened, not the memory erased. She'd made it in three minutes, hands moving without thought — the way you tie a shoe, the way you breathe.

The tin lid closed with a soft thud.

Vinegar and the mineral tang of copper. The shop smelled the way it always did, the scent so familiar she barely noticed it until something changed. Behind her, shelves of bottles caught the lamplight — dark glass, clear glass, some so old the labels had yellowed. She knew what each one held.

She carried the mortar to the back room, ran water from the pump, wiped the residue with a cloth, then rinsed the cloth and draped it over the sink's edge.

When she returned to the front counter, the small bell on the shelf behind the register rang — not the broken one above the door, but the one that meant a customer had entered the shop proper. She hadn't rung it herself. It must have been the motion of the door.

The man standing in the doorway wore a dark coat, well-made but worn at the cuffs. His hands were in his pockets. His face held the stillness of someone who had decided, finally, to walk in.

"We're closing," Maren said.

"I know." His voice was flat, past courtesy. "I just need one thing."

"We open at eight tomorrow."

"This won't wait until tomorrow."

Maren studied him. The lamp above the register cast its warm circle on the wood between them. Outside, dusk had bled into dark glass. She could have refused. The sign on the door said the shop closed at dusk, and dusk had passed.

She didn't.

"What do you need?"

The man took his hands from his pockets. His fingers were long, the knuckles rough. He looked at the shelves behind her, at the mortar and pestle on the counter, at the row of ingredients in small clay pots along the back wall—charcoal, vinegar, pale powder, dried herbs.

"The grief potion," he said.

Maren's expression didn't change. She had heard the request before, many times. The words that usually followed — *my mother*, *my father*, *my friend* — and the ones that sometimes followed — *my child*, *my lover*, *my spouse*. She had made the potion for all of them.

"You know the terms," she said.

"I do."

"Memory stays. Emotion goes. You'll remember her name, her face, what happened — you won't feel it."

"I know."

"You can't undo it."

"I know."

Maren reached for the clay pot nearest her hand. Charcoal. She ran her thumb along the rim. "Sit down," she said, nodding toward the stool by the counter. "Tell me about her."

Elias hesitated. Then he pulled the stool out from under the counter and sat. The wood creaked under his weight.

"What's your name?" Maren asked.

"Elias."

"Elias." She said it once, then turned to the counter and laid out the ingredients — charcoal, vinegar, pale powder, a small bottle of something clear she kept in a locked drawer. Her hands found each item without searching. "Tell me about her, Elias."

Elias looked at the ingredients she was arranging and at her hands, steady and precise.

"She died," he said.

Maren's hands paused. Just for a moment. Then they resumed their work.

"When?" she asked.

"Six months ago. A fever. It came on fast — she was fine in the morning and dead by nightfall."


Maren said nothing. She picked up the mortar and pestle and began to measure the charcoal, her movements slow and deliberate. The pestle made a soft sound against the stone.

"You want to forget her," Maren said. Not a question.

"I want to forget how it feels."

"Those are the same thing."

Elias looked at her, expression shifting for the first time. "Are you going to help me, or lecture me?"

Maren set down the pestle. She looked at him across the counter. The lamp above the register threw its warm circle on the wood between them.

"I'm going to help you," she said. "But you need to understand what you're asking for."

"I've already—"

"You'll remember her name and face, and that she died. You won't remember why it mattered."

Elias said nothing.

"You'll look at the spot where she slept and feel nothing. You'll hear her name and feel nothing. You'll carry the facts of her — weight without warmth."

"I know."

"Do you?"

The question hung in the air. The shop was very quiet.

Elias looked at the laid-out ingredients and at her hands waiting on the counter.

"She used to sing," he said. "When she was cooking. She'd sing these stupid songs — made-up words, melodies that didn't go anywhere." He paused. "I can't remember the last time I heard her sing."

Maren said nothing.

"I can't remember the last thing she said to me. I can't remember —" His voice broke, then flattened. "I can't remember the last time I told her I loved her."

Maren reached across the counter and laid her hand over his. Just for a moment. Then she drew it back.

"I won't make it tonight," she said. "Drink water. Eat something. Come back tomorrow at eight."

"Is that your answer?"

"That's my answer."

Elias looked at her. Something raw had opened behind the flatness.

"You'll make it?" he asked.

"I'll make it."

He stood. The stool scraped against the floor. He turned toward the door, then stopped.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me yet," Maren said.

Elias opened the door. He stepped out into the dark, and the door closed behind him.

Maren stood behind the counter for a long time. The shop was quiet. The lamp above the register threw its warm circle on the wood. The ingredients lay on the counter, waiting.

She looked at the shelf above the register, at the bottle she never touched — the one she'd made for herself years ago and never used. The glass was dark, the label yellowed. She knew what it held.

She turned off the lamp and stood in the dark. The shop smelled the way it always did. The street outside was quiet.

She didn't touch the bottle.
