# The Potion Maker

Measured into the mortar, the charcoal made a dark circle against the stone.

"How long since she died?" Maren asked.

"Six months."

"Other things you've tried? Tonics, sleep draughts, anything?"

"Everything the apothecary had." Elias's fingers found the counter's edge and traced it, back and forth. "Nothing touches it."

Three drops of vinegar into the powder. The mixture darkened, releasing a sharp mineral smell that cut through the shop's permanent layer of herbs and old wood.

"Have you had the grief potion before? From another maker?"

"Once. Two years ago. For my father."

A pause. Then: "And?"

"And it worked. I remembered him, but the—" He stopped. "The weight went away."

The pestle moved in slow circles against the mortar, grinding the mixture smooth. A soft, steady sound.

"When did you last see her?"

"The morning she died. She was making breakfast. Flour on her hands." Flat now, reciting facts. "I kissed her forehead and told her I'd be home by supper. By the time I got back, she was gone."

Around and around the pestle went.

"Her name?"

"Lena."

Nothing from Maren. She set down the pestle, reached for the bone ash—pale powder, cool against her fingers—and measured a pinch into the mixture. Stirred.

Outside, full dark had settled. The windows were black mirrors, catching the lamplight, the shelves, the two of them at the counter.

"What do you remember about her?"

The question came from somewhere behind her professional distance. She hadn't planned it.

Elias looked down at his hands. They'd stopped moving.

"She burned toast every morning. Every single morning. The smoke alarm would go off and she'd wave a dish towel at it and laugh." His voice was still flat, but something had shifted beneath it. "She had a scar on her left hand—from when she was twelve. Said she fell off a bike, but her sister told me later she'd tried to steal a cookie from the oven."

Maren's hands had stopped too.

Still now, the pestle in the mortar, the mixture unfinished. The smell of vinegar and bone ash filling the space between them.

"Rainy days," Elias said. "She hated them—not because she didn't like the rain. She said it made everything look tired. She'd stand at the window and watch people walking by and say, 'Look at them. They've all forgotten how to be wet.'"

Against the counter's edge, Maren stared. Lamplight catching the pestle's rim, the mortar's edge, the line where wood met stone.

"You loved her," Maren said. Not a question.

"More than anything."

"And you want to forget that."

"I want to forget the way it feels to remember her."

"That's the same thing."

"Is it?" Elias looked up. His eyes were dry, but something raw had opened behind them. "When I remember her laugh, I feel it in my chest. When I remember she's dead, I can't breathe. Those are two different things."

The binding agent next—clear liquid, uncorked, three drops measured into the mortar. The mixture shimmered, then settled.

"I'll need to prepare the ingredients," Maren said. "It will take time."

"How long?"

"Tonight. Come back in an hour."

"You'll have it ready?"

The lamp above the register threw its warm circle on the wood between them. The ingredients lay in the mortar, waiting.

"I'll have it ready," she said.

He stood. The stool scraped against the floor. A coin—silver, heavy—landed on the counter.

"That's not necessary."

"Consider it a deposit."

She picked it up, dropped it in the tin beneath the register.

Elias moved toward the door, then stopped with his hand on the frame. "What's your name?"

"Maren."

"Maren." Once, the way she'd said his name. Then the door opened and he stepped into the dark. The bell above the register didn't ring.

Alone now. The shop quiet. The ingredients in the mortar, unfinished. The lamp throwing its circle on the wood.

On the shelf above the register, the bottle she never touched—dark glass, yellowed label. She knew what it held.

She turned to the back room.

The workbench was bare except for empty jars and a cloth draped over the pump handle. She set the mortar down and stood there, palms flat against the wood, breathing.

Vinegar. Herbs. The metallic edge of something old. The shop smelled the same as it always did.

She didn't reach for the jars. Didn't open the drawers. Just stood, hands on the counter, looking at nothing.

Then the first jar, unscrewed.
