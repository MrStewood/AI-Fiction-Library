# The Potion Maker

Above the register, the bell rang once.

Through the door came Elias, his coat dark against the lamplight, the same worn cuffs, the same stillness in his face — but something in the set of his shoulders had shifted. Out there in the dark for an hour, deciding.

Already at the counter, Maren had the ingredients laid out in a neat row: ground charcoal in a shallow dish, vinegar in a stoppered vial, bone ash pale as chalk dust in a folded paper, the binding agent — clear, almost invisible — in a small glass bottle. Ordinary things. The kind of items that could stock a kitchen shelf.

"Sit down."

The stool scraped as Elias pulled it out and sat. Wood creaked under his weight. He looked at the ingredients but didn't touch them.

"These are the base materials." Maren picked up the dish of charcoal and set it closer to him. "Charcoal. Vinegar. Bone ash. The binding agent." One finger touched the glass bottle. "Nothing in this row costs more than a few coppers."

A nod from Elias.

"The potion is simple to make. The materials are simple. That's not the cost."

Her hands rested on the counter, palms down, steady.

"What it takes from you — that's the cost. Not the grief. The grief goes, that's the point. But the grief doesn't travel alone. Every memory connected to it, every moment that gave the grief its shape, those lose their weight too. You'll remember the events. You won't remember why they mattered."

Quiet now. The lamp above the register threw its circle on the wood between them. Outside, full dark had settled hours ago, the windows gone to black mirrors.

"You'll remember her name, her face, the years you shared. You'll remember that she died. What you won't remember is what it felt like to love her."

Flat on the counter, Elias's hands mirrored hers. He didn't look away.

"I understand."

"Do you? When you think of her now, there's pain. After the potion, there'll be nothing — not relief, not peace. Nothing. The memories will be there, but they'll be facts. Dates. Names. The weight goes, and the warmth goes with it."

"I understand."

"Can you be more specific? Tell me what you think will change."

At the ingredients, Elias looked. At the charcoal's dark surface, the vinegar's sharp edge, the bone ash's pale, fine dust.

"She made bread on Sundays," he said. "Every Sunday. The kitchen would smell like flour and yeast. I'd come in from the yard and she'd have flour on her hands and she'd say —" A pause. Then, quieter: "After the potion, I'll remember that she made bread on Sundays. I won't remember the smell."

"That's right."

"I'll remember her scar. I won't remember how I felt when I saw it."

"That's right."

Near the edge of the counter, the pestle sat in the mortar. Maren's hand found it, resting there without moving.

"Will you remember her name?"

"Her name is Lena."

"That's not what I asked."

Up now, Elias's eyes. Dry, the flatness gone, what lay behind it raw and unguarded.

"I will remember everything," he said. "I just won't feel it."

The words hung in the space between them. On the pestle, Maren's hand tightened — just slightly, the knuckles whitening — then set it down.

Above the register, the bottle on the high shelf caught the lamplight. Dark glass. Yellowed label. It sat where it had sat for years, untouched, gathering a thin skin of dust that Maren wiped away each morning without thinking about what it held.

Still on the counter, her hands.

"Have you ever made one of these for yourself?"

From nowhere, the question. Not from his mouth — from hers. She'd heard herself ask it before she knew she was going to speak.

Elias looked at her for a long time. The lamp burned between them.

"No. That's your job."

Without moving, Maren's hands stayed on the counter. The ingredients waited in their row — charcoal, vinegar, bone ash, binding agent. Ordinary things. The cost named and waiting to be paid.

Across the counter, across the lamplight, across the small space where the ingredients sat, she looked at Elias. No longer just a customer. A man who had named his dead wife's bread, her scar, the way she laughed at smoke alarms. A man about to lose all of it — not the memories, but the weight that made them mean something.

And she was the one who would take it from him.

"Tomorrow night. Come back at dusk. I'll have the potion ready."

"Tonight—"

"Tomorrow."

Elias stood. The stool scraped. He looked at the ingredients one more time, then at Maren, then at the door.

"Tomorrow."

The bell rang once as he left. The door closed. The shop was quiet.

At the counter, Maren stood. Palms down on the wood, still. The ingredients waited in their neat row, the lamp throwing its warm circle on the counter, on her hands, on the space where Elias had been.

The bottle on the high shelf looked down. She didn't look up.

With the mortar in her hands, she turned to the back room.
