# The Potion Maker

Between them, the bottle waited. Clear glass catching the lamplight, the liquid inside still and ordinary. Elias stared at it the way a man stares at a door he is not sure he should open.

Along the walls, shelves held their dark bottles in neat rows. Jars of bone ash and dried herbs crowded the counter. Years of hands doing this same work had worn the wood smooth.

Finding the glass with his fingers, he lifted it to eye level and held it there — light passing through, the shelves behind it blurring through liquid and glass. Then, looking at Maren, he paused.

She said nothing. Her hands rested on the counter, palms down, still. Not helping. Not stopping.

The bottle came to his lips. His head tipped back. The liquid slid down his throat — a quick, easy motion, like drinking water after thirst. He swallowed.

For a moment, nothing happened. The bottle set down on the counter with a small, ordinary sound against the wood.

Then — not all at once, not dramatically, but with the quiet inevitability of a tide going out — something left him. His face did not change. His posture did not shift. But behind his eyes, the space settled, like sediment finding the bottom of a still pond. The lines around his mouth softened. Not into a smile — into nothing. Into a face that had stopped holding something it no longer needed to hold.

At the shop, he looked. Shelves of bottles. Jars of ingredients. The counter. The lamp.

At Maren, he looked.

"The street's wet," he said. "Rained earlier."

She nodded.

The empty bottle turned in his fingers — ordinary glass, nothing special — then set back down. He stood. The chair scraped against the floorboards, a sharp, sudden sound in the quiet.

"Thank you," he said.

The door bell rang — bright, cheerful, absurd in the silence — and then he was gone, and the street was dark.

The shop was quiet.

The empty bottle sat where Elias had left it, catching the lamplight. She looked at it for a long time.

Above the register, on the high shelf where it had sat for years, the dark bottle with its yellowed label waited. Her fingers found the glass — cool, dusty, familiar. She drew it down, held it in both hands. The label was faded, the writing barely legible. She had made this years ago, after the worst of it, when the shop was new and her hands still shook.

Her thumb found the stopper. Pressed against it. The wax seal was old, brittle — it would crack easily. One pull, and it would open.

The glass warmed against her palms.

She set it back on the shelf. The sound it made — glass on wood, a small, precise clink — was the loudest thing in the shop.

The door frame came cool against her palm. She stood in the threshold, looking out. Wet cobblestones caught the light from a distant lamp. The air smelled of rain and old stone and, behind her, the herbs and vinegar and mineral sharpness of the shop.

The empty bottle from Elias's drink was light in her hand. Nothing.

She did not raise it. She did not drop it.

On the threshold between the warm light and the dark street, she held what she would not set down.
