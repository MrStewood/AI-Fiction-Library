# Target Reader Review — SCENE-001

## Reading-Experience Summary

As a target reader — a literary fantasy reader who enjoys contemplative, character-driven stories with grounded magic, the kind who reads Kelly Link, Sofia Samatar, Raymond Carver — I finished SCENE-001 and felt the pull to continue. The opening is quiet, controlled, and assured. It delivers exactly what the brief promises at this gate: a world I understand, a protagonist whose competence and restraint I trust, and a request that lands with personal weight.

The story opens in media res with the tail end of a routine transaction, then introduces Elias and his specific, devastating request. The pacing is deliberate but never sluggish. The prose is close, intimate, concrete. I feel the shop — the smell of rosemary and vinegar, the texture of the clay pots, the warm circle of lamplight. This is a writer who trusts the reader to infer from action and image rather than being told.

By the end of the scene, I know what Maren does, how her magic works, what Elias wants, and — critically — I've seen the first crack in her professional armor. The hand she places over his, then pulls back. The bottle on the shelf she never touches. The story promise is clearly visible: this is a story about grief, choice, and cost, and it will be hard and honest.

## Strongest Elements

1. **Sensory grounding that earns its place.** The shop smells like rosemary, vinegar, copper. The ingredients have texture (charcoal, pale powder, a small bottle from a locked drawer). The lamp casts a warm circle. None of this is decorative — it establishes Maren's world as real, tactile, and precise. The reader feels the shop. This is the grounded magic the brief promises.

2. **Subtext in dialogue.** Elias's "I want to forget how it feels" vs. Maren's "Those are the same thing" is the scene's best exchange. It names the central question without answering it. The exchange about the wife's singing — "stupid songs, made-up words, melodies that didn't go anywhere" — is the scene's emotional peak. It makes the reader feel what Elias is losing without sentimentality.

3. **Maren's competence and restraint.** The brief promises a protagonist who has agency and whose choice is the engine. Maren's hands move without searching. She lays out ingredients precisely. She tells Elias to come back tomorrow — not no, not yet. This is a person who controls the pace. The reader trusts her. The bottle on the shelf at the end — the one she made for herself and never used — is planted without explanation. It works. I want to know.

4. **The departing customer as quiet world-building.** "A half-measure. He'd wanted the edges softened, not the memory erased." This establishes the magic system's range in two sentences, without exposition. The reader understands the spectrum before Elias walks in.

5. **The closing image.** "She didn't touch the bottle." Simple, final, resonant. The scene ends where it should — with Maren alone in the dark, the ingredients waiting, the choice suspended.

## Drop-Off Risks

1. **Low — the opening is strong.** For the target reader, this scene delivers. The prose is controlled, the world is clear, the conflict is specific and personal. There is no info-dump, no sentimentality, no tidy moral. The reader knows what's at stake and wants to see where it goes.

2. **Minor risk: the "come back tomorrow" deferral.** Maren sends Elias away rather than committing to make the potion or refusing. This is appropriate — it's a professional assessment protocol — but the reader has been promised that the story's engine starts here, and the deferral could feel like a delay. The scene contract says the turning point is the specificity of the request, and the outcome is Maren's equilibrium disrupted. Both land. But the reader is left waiting. For a short story with a compressed timeline, this is a minor structural tension, not a flaw.

3. **Minor risk: the unnamed bottle.** The bottle on the shelf is planted without explanation. For the target reader, this is intriguing — it signals that Maren has her own history of loss. For a less patient reader, it could feel like a tease. But the brief targets literary fantasy readers who enjoy moral texture. This works for that audience.

## Confusion Points

1. **The two bells.** The scene specifies that the small bell on the shelf behind the register rang — "not the broken one above the door." This is clear enough, but the two-bell setup could benefit from one sentence of grounding earlier in the scene (e.g., Maren noticing the broken bell above the door when she first enters). Currently, the broken bell is mentioned only in passing, and the reader might wonder why there are two bells. This is a minor clarity issue, not a confusion point.

2. **The locked-drawer bottle.** Maren reaches for "a small bottle of something clear she kept in a locked drawer." The implication is that this is a rare or powerful ingredient — perhaps the key component of the grief potion. But the scene does not explain what it is or why it's locked. For the target reader, this is world-building through implication. It works. But the reader should be aware that the bottle's significance is withheld by design — it's setup, not confusion.

3. **The departing customer's identity.** The brief requires two speaking characters only (REQ-001). The departing customer is non-speaking and unnamed. He is functional, not a character. The scene contract confirms this. No confusion.

## Verdict

**PASS**

The scene delivers on all required elements for this checkpoint. The prose is controlled, the world is grounded, the characters are compelling, and the story promise is visible. The minor issues (two-bell clarity, deferral tension) are not flaws — they are the texture of a compressed opening. No revision required.

## Notes for Later Stages

- **Continuity watch:** The two bells should be grounded earlier in the scene if possible, or the "broken one above the door" should be introduced before the bell rings.
- **Setup payoff:** The bottle on the shelf is the scene's most important planted detail. It must pay off in the story's second half — not before.
- **The locked-drawer bottle:** The reader will expect this ingredient to matter. If it's the key to the grief potion, its significance should be revealed when the potion is prepared (likely SCENE-002 or SCENE-003).
- **Elias's singing memory:** This is the scene's emotional peak. It should echo later — perhaps in Maren's own memory, or in the final choice.
